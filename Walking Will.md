### Walking Will

Michael's House: described on Google, "a haven of hope and healing" in Palm Springs. I can't speak to its claim directly, but from the street I felt its pull.

The first time I walked by, I was nearing the end of my journey through addiction, stubborn in my good nature and rebellion. I had been holding tight onto the notion that the current systems intending to help are failing those of us who need help the most. In fact, I suspected people experiencing harm from the compounded consequences of poverty and hard drug use were also most likely to have additional trauma from the treatment centers and hospitals that ostensibly are here to help them.

Will Rogers liked to say that he never met a stranger that he didn’t like. There is a quiet part of that sentiment: the operative word was “met.” Will didn't get along with anybody and everybody— it was that he gave anybody a proper chance and everybody a decent shot.

As for me, beset by a turbulent childhood in small-town Oklahoma and an instinct for survival, I stayed far away from folk flagging pipes and needles, in judgment of their actions and fear of how they'd affect my own. I had my eyes set on fortune and freedom. From my calculations and my penchant for people pleasing, walking full Will and achieving my dreams were rather incompatible.

That night early in my rocky (near bottomy) Palm Springs landing, I walked slowly by Michael’s House, making eye contact with a man who had walked out of the complex of bushes and buildings to have a cigarette. I felt like a traveling lone stranger who had come from far away, seeing something like the destination and wondering if he was seeing a mirage in the desert.

It wasn’t but a few weeks later that I had smashed my bubble, grabbed my canvas backpack out of the back of my grubby Subaru, and walked with a kind of sure foot requiring fanfare in my headphones. At 4 AM on a cool February morning, I walked up to the office door, found it locked, and set my ass down with my bag. I felt a cool breeze, and relief came over me. Then a calling.

The tarot calling.

I had been a software engineer in a past life, but during these last few years I picked up astrology and tarot through a series of unlikely events. They were my close accomplices in a time of extreme isolation due to stigma and my annoying habit of living in authenticity, even during active use.

I began shuffling and sorting, practicing my own gremlin art honed through grit and a lack of living human teachers. I don’t remember the faces of the cards spilling urgently out of my hand, except for one: the moon. I held it in my hand, out of a new deck with edges mint condition. I held it, hoping for some sort of spirit osmosis, something to resolve the dissonance I felt with my physical reality. The emerging spiritual insight was disconcerting to me.

It wasn’t time yet. And this wasn’t my path.

Later on, the physical reality of the situation would come into full clarity. I did not have health insurance in California yet, so there’s no way that a recovery community like Michael’s House could have clicked in place for me at that time. I was meant to come to the conclusion a couple weeks later that I was done with crystal methamphetamine for good, that my decision wasn't about surviving but about purpose. I would be the leader of my own personal revolution. That I was strong enough, and skillful enough, and resilient enough, to be in my small solitude for a little longer.

I wasn’t meant to have that Michael's House story. That said, a few weeks into my resolve now, I like to walk by there from time to time. It gives me a feeling of warmth and shelter. Sometimes the people and places in our lives don’t serve the obvious functions that we are programmed to look for.

Sometimes, we have to look for that glimmer of an invisible string, and know we weren't failed by the system. We were given a journey of learning that we are our own answer.
