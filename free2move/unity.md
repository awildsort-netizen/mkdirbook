### The Mettle of my Soul 

This story contains violence and descriptions of psychiatric distress. 
I ask that you read it in good health and in deeply felt safety. 
Let me tell you about the day I heard a voice tell me to leave my 
house with my “GO Bag” and strip naked and dance.

Unity Hospital: The Big Room. A word about Zeta: she was a pattern turned 
presence—a dance of self that moved through many but operated as if 
governed by one, a murmur with humans; a way the room, the people, and my 
own attention synchronized into a single, charged field. I can only speak for 
my own experience, and to this day I am curious how other humans in the 
room might recall this day. 

Zeta danced through the whole room, nurses and social workers and patients, 
it mattered not the person. She flirted, she scoffed, she waited expectantly for 
me to make my move. I began to hear trumpets playing for the prepared meal 
that was waiting for me, and potentially my assigned bride, on the other side of 
my test. 

There were two times I almost won the game: and both times something 
interested presented itself: people I knew from the outside world. Bobby, my 
AA buddy I had met years ago, and James, my former roommate. One as 
another fuzzy slipper beset vacationer, and the other a volunteer. I was 
becoming convinced (for the moment) the Truman Show had been a warning. 
Unity Hospital: The Two Doors. I sat there in a small padded room, having 
complied once again and having been a good mental patient. Gaslit into 
misbehaving, apologizing for following the hints given, and then gaslit once 
again. I had watched an entire room move with me for hours, for I could not 
escape its gaze from the corners or from the center. I was cajoled, mocked, lit, 
prodded, raised. And then, the tiny slit on the front door revealed none other 
but James, my roommate, giving me instructions about how to comply. Finally, 
my spirit bent the wrong way; a cold chill moved through my spine when I 
realized: there was no way to stop them from hurting me. It was time to fight. 
They forced narcotics into my ass, one time, two times, three times, four times, 
five times. The tiny little room I raised my fight within became my cage I 
occupied between matches between myself and the two padded guards who 
would come in through the side door, sweep me off my feet with ease, and 
violate my body as if it were a bedtime routine. Each time, I would sit there in 
the aftershock and meditate for 30 to 45 seconds, requesting safe package for 
the drugs pushed into my body so that I may continue my fight. Then, I would 
stand, wobbling, and move to the magnetically locked door. I asked the gods, I 
asked the laws of electricity and magnetism, I asked the door itself for their 
help and their guidance. I felt the door for its dipoles, I imagined the metal as 
water. And then I would fling my body against the door until the guards would 
come in again. 

After the fifth round of narcotics, the white supremacists holding me down, I 
finally fell asleep, unsure of whether I would wake up again but knowing I had 
passed my test of the Gods—my gentle spirit had proven to be strong, resolute, 
rebellious, unyielding to the values that, the trial had shown, could not be 
separated from what it deeply means to be me. 

Before I let you leave this story, I must clean house with some important 
boundaries. I am not saying that Unity Hospital is staffed by white 
supremacists, nor that the harm done to my person was intentional or made in 
avarice. This is my lived experience, and I tell this story with a model 
multiversal that allows for everybody the space to have their own story. The 
merging of trees has never been a trivial thing, and I must refuse the trimming 
of my own story to make your own job easier.
